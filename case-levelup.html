LevelUp: a game that runs on business logic

About 9 minutes to read

LevelUp is a gamification platform for TaskUs operations floors. Managers turn a KPI into a timed challenge. Agents compete as their own animated 3D characters on live leaderboards, on their desktops and on the floor TVs. Every sale gets a celebration, and everyone who takes part wins something.

FactDetail
RoleLead Product Designer and the only designer: planning, research, design, demos, handover and visual QA
TeamA senior director, our SVP as reviewer, a senior development manager, a project manager, front-end and back-end developers, testers
TimelineFirst design in about a week. Launched in January 2024
PlatformWeb, on agent, manager and operations manager desktops, and on floor TVs during live challenges

The brief

Managers on the operations floors wanted a gamification tool to motivate their teams. I had tried gamification twice before, at different times. Each time, the work went cold and the tool moved down the priority list. I felt those attempts were not convincing enough to stick.

Then our senior director came to me with a different question: can you work with 3D objects and make something that feels like a game but works on business logic?

I joined client calls to hear what they wanted in their own words. One client told us to play and watch Farlight 84, a hero shooter, and asked for LevelUp to look as close to it as possible.

My usual order, reversed

On products like EvaluateUs, I launch a first version with the priority features only, simple enough to use without help. Character comes later: more colour, micro-interactions, delight, and more complex flows as the product grows with its users, the way Figma did.

LevelUp needed the opposite. I studied Zoho's gamification and other tools. They asked users to reach many complex goals, and they still looked like business software. I did not want that. So I gave the visuals priority over complex features that nobody would use. LevelUp was never meant to feel like SaaS or an ERP. It was meant to be as much of a game as it could be.

Research: a game is not a business app

Before I designed a screen, I read about how games work: how they hook players and which components they use. My references were Asphalt, other opponent challenge games, and the game the client gave us. Three findings changed how I designed:

  • Everything is bold. In a game, every button and every screen is flashy, with light and continuous animation around it. So LevelUp tables glow, and celebration screens have animation and planned sound.
  • Games take more clicks, on purpose. A business app changes a date range with a dropdown. In LevelUp, a date range change or a profile switch covers the full screen. This slower pace gives players room to breathe and get ready.
  • Fixed frames are fine. Business apps must fit every screen. LevelUp supports a small set of screen sizes, so components keep the right scale and perspective, as they do in a game.

Everyone wins

This was the one decision we made on purpose, and the idea came from me. Games are cut-throat. A business tool cannot be. No manager wants a teammate to feel pitied because they did not perform. If that happens, the tool fails at its purpose.

LevelUp exists to motivate everyone and to celebrate every win: personal, team and company. So in a sales challenge, a teammate still gets something to celebrate even if they have not made a sale yet.

Who it serves

  • Admins and team managers create the games: the KPI to improve (for example sales or upsell), the objective, points per sale, the winning criteria, the teams and the time.
  • Agents ("Teammates" in the product) play. They get an invite, pick a character and compete.
  • Operations managers and site leaders watch adoption and engagement on a dashboard.

Key design decisions

1. Your own animated character

While exploring, I found Ready Player Me, a free custom character builder at the time. Our developers converted those characters into fully rigged Three.js characters that dance, wave and act out dozens of other moves to celebrate a win. I also converted and tested assets in Three.js myself, then gave the developers refined code they could embed. Agents pick from 18 characters, 9 male and 9 female, on their first login, and can change any time. Before today's AI tools, this level of engineering would have been very hard for a team our size.

2. One KPI model for every campaign

A KPI has a name, a unit (number or percent) and a direction: higher is better, or lower is better. Sales go up; AHT goes down. With this one rule, any campaign KPI can become a challenge, with no custom build.

3. A wizard that shows the game while you build it

Creating a challenge takes three steps: Basic, Goal Settings and Rewards. A live challenge card on the right shows exactly what agents will see. When the manager saves, the system enrols every teammate in the selected teams and sends each one an invite. A first-time player picks a character before they join.

4. Rewards for more than the top three

The rewards step has two rows. Traditional milestones cover 1st, 2nd and 3rd position. Motivational rewards cover everyone else: Super Starter for the first sale in the grace period, Best Customer Feedback for quality, and Above and Beyond for extra effort. I wrote ten badge ideas, each with a purpose, so newer and improving agents can win too.

5. Celebrate on the floor, in the moment

Every sale sets off a grand celebration on screen. Every upsell can make you the MVP. When a challenge ends, the winners go into the Hall of Fame. Each celebration closes itself after a short countdown, so it never blocks work.

6. Status that is always in view

The agent's card (name, level, progress to the next level) sits top left on every screen. The running challenge and its countdown sit top centre. An agent never has to look for where they stand.

The admin side: a game managers run alone

The admin console was built first, so each client instance could be set up and tested before the game screens. It keeps LevelUp running without help from the product team.

  • Groups by campaign, LOB and team, each with its own KPIs and rewards.
  • KPIs with a unit and a direction, reused across challenges.
  • Teams and players, with a leader for each team.
  • Bulk upload of KPI values, with a notification when an upload is done.
  • Roles, so each site controls who can create and change challenges.
  • Reports per player and challenge: target, KPI value, points earned and MVP.

Every list shares the same filters: date presets (today, last week, last month, custom), campaign, LOB and site.

Designed to be measured

I designed the leadership dashboard around two questions: are people logging in, and are they playing? The dashboard defines its two headline measures in plain words, on the screen:

  • Adoption: users who logged in, out of all users provisioned.
  • Engagement: users who took part in challenges, out of all users provisioned.

Next to them are challenge status (running, finished, aborted, scheduled), KPI trends over time and the overall leaderboard. It also covers the states people often skip: skeleton loaders while data loads, a clear message for empty results, and a clear error message.

Craft: a game look on a business frame

After I studied each type of screen a game has, I set a style scheme and followed it everywhere: glowing tables, neon on a dark base, HUD-style panels and bold celebration type. Under the look sits a strict structure, with one navigation, one header, and the same filters and tables on every admin screen.

LevelUp got its own component library and design system, so every screen kept the same components and styling: navigation items with default, hover and selected states, tabs, dropdowns, challenge cards with a countdown, badge sets, avatar circles and badge animations. Type is Exo 2 for game moments and Poppins for data.

Delivery: protecting the fun

I knew the flashy parts would meet friction. Developers are usually asked to ship features on time, not to spend days perfecting one celebration screen. So I had to be both firm and bold to get what I designed into the build.

I sat with the developers whenever they had questions or ideas, and I learned a lot from them. With the testers, I flagged every UI and visual difference next to their functional feedback, so the look was tested as seriously as the logic. A product like this only ships when the whole team is on the same page.

Outcome

In the first client demo, the client was so happy that they signed up the same day. Eight priority clients that had asked for a similar tool followed. LevelUp ran on agent, manager and operations manager desktops, and on the floor TVs during live challenges. It was the first gamification system of its kind at TaskUs and became the company standard.

Our SVP presented LevelUp at AWS Summit London 2025. The audience gave it a big round of applause for bringing something to corporate software that nobody had seen before.

Why it mattered to me

My first job was in a call centre, in 2015, while I was still in college. The call-tagging system was so complex that agents had to memorise the categories, write them down or look them up, while they were already on the next call. In my first weeks, I asked a senior for help with tagging every day, and I kept notes on each call so I could tag it later.

When I joined TaskUs in 2021, the industry had moved a long way, but the tools agents use had not moved as much. Many agents come to the floor from travel, delivery, cinema or social media, not from Power BI or Jira. Put an ERP in front of them and they switch off. A frustrated, unmotivated employee is one of the biggest costs a business can carry.

As the first and only designer in Digital IT, I raised the standard step by step, from complexity to simplicity: let the user focus on the one thing they came to do on each screen, and design from their experience, not ours. LevelUp is where that thinking went furthest.

What I learned

No effort is wasted. My two earlier gamification attempts prepared me for this one.

Games also taught me something about business: when a company thinks about every side of its teammates' day, the floor changes. Challenge days came with prizes, and they were the most energetic days we had. I heard teammates in the lifts and in the cafeteria talking about their characters' stats and how many times they had made the Hall of Fame.

I have always wanted to make things people use. Early in my career, I proudly pointed out logos I had designed on the front of buildings. Now my work is on thousands of screens every day. The business has to focus on operations. My job was to give the best experience to the people who use TaskUs products, because often nobody else is thinking about them.

When LevelUp launched, even as an MVP, the whole team could not believe what we had achieved. It changed how we worked. My senior director and our SVPs had seen what a great experience can do, and in our next products they pushed for the best possible experience from the first version. They saw that good user experience brings business.
