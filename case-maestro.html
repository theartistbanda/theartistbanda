Maestro: your team's numbers, in the chat you already use

About 9 minutes to read

Maestro is an AI assistant I designed at TaskUs. It lives inside Google Chat. Team leads, operations managers and client QAs ask it a question in plain words, for example: "Who are the team members with a schedule adherence of less than 90%?" Maestro reads the business data behind tools like Power BI, Salesforce, ServiceNow and Zendesk. It replies with the number, the people behind it, the likely cause, and a next step: coach, give kudos, or open the source.

The brief

TaskUs was moving off Google products. It was a long journey, and Gmail and Google Chat were the last tools that teams still used every day. Most TaskUs clients used them too. So when clients asked to reach their business data in chat, Google Chat was the place to build it.

Our DVP came to me and said we needed to design a conversation. He added the details and shared some documents. Then I started my research.

The need was easy to say. A team lead's day turns on a few numbers: schedule attainment, adherence, average handle time (AHT) and customer satisfaction (CSAT). That data sat in many systems. A team lead who wanted to know who was late today, and how many times this week, had to dig for it before they could plan a training session. Maestro had to put that information at their fingertips.

Research: how people actually talk

First, I looked for products that did something like this. At the time, I found almost nothing to learn from.

So I went back to the basics: how do people talk to each other? I read about tone, about length, and about the balance between formal and casual. Then I looked at how most of our users wrote. They were not formal. They were casual, and they used very few words.

The devil is in the details, so I gave Maestro the same tone. It says "Good Morning! Kudos!" and "Hey! ... Reach out and make sure everything is okay!" It is a little casual, and it never uses more words than it needs.

The questions came from the users

The first version had to answer the questions team leads and managers really ask. I collected the questions managers ask most often, and their most recent queries. Then I shared a researched list back with them, and they chose the questions they wanted to keep. For example:

  • "Who are the 5 agents with Break and Lunch % more than 15% for the past week?"
  • "Which of my TMs are consistently missing Schedule Attainment target for 2 weeks?"
  • "What has been my team's absenteeism for past 4 weeks?"

Two flows: told, or asked

The framework splits Maestro into two flows.

Proactive. Maestro speaks first, based on how the team lead (or their admin) set it up. For example: a summary of last week at the start of the shift, an update every 1 or 2 hours on chosen KPIs, or an alert when a number moves in an unusual way.

On demand. The team lead asks a question. Maestro answers, and the team lead asks follow-up questions until they have what they need.

Designing inside Google Chat

Maestro had to run in Google Chat, so it had to follow Google Chat's rules. I wanted to make the build as easy as possible for the developers. So I recreated the Google Chat interface in Figma, and I designed our conversations, tables and charts inside it. Developers saw exactly what they would build, in the place where it would live.

Google Chat has many limits on what a message can hold. Buttons, icons inside buttons, tables and charts all took a lot of R&D with the developers to find out what was possible. The design changed as we learned. That is why every version could be built.

One answer pattern for every question

In a chat app, it is easy to give every answer a different shape. Then users must read each reply from the start. I set one pattern, so the same kind of information always looks the same:

  1. What Maestro understood. Each answer repeats the question, the date range, the campaign and the result. The team lead can see at once if Maestro read the question correctly.
  2. The people. A short table shows the agents behind the number.
  3. Insights. One line for each agent, with the likely cause.
  4. What to do. A short recommendation, with links to the policy or dashboard behind it. Each link shows its source, for example Glowstick or Power BI.
  5. Act here. A button on each row: Coaching Alert for agents who need help, Kudos for agents who did well.
  6. Feedback. Thumbs up and thumbs down under every answer.

Key design decisions

1. Start the shift with a brief, not a dashboard

The morning message opens with good news: "Good Morning! Kudos! Huge shout out to your team for their stellar performance last week!" Then it goes through four KPIs in a fixed order: schedule attainment, adherence, AHT and CSAT. Most sections give the team result against the target, how many people missed it, and the bottom 3.

2. Praise and coaching in the same message

Agents who met the target get a Kudos button on their row, which adds XOXO points. Agents below the target get a Coaching Alert button, which tags them for coaching in Boost. Both take one tap, so thanks and coaching do not become tasks for later.

3. Causes a team lead can act on

The early insights were vague, for example: "Extended conversation duration for Alex Turner." I made each line say which part of the number moved, by how much, and over what period:

  • "Alex Turner had higher talk time by 20 seconds over the last 2 weeks."
  • "Ethan Hawke had higher hold time by 30 seconds over the last 2 weeks."
  • "Liam Gallagher had higher ACW by 15 seconds over the last 2 weeks."

The same AHT number now shows three different coaching needs.

4. Training for the person, not the team

The first version suggested three general courses for everyone, such as "Leadership Essentials: Navigating the Corporate Landscape." The later version links one course to each agent, based on their weak score. For example: "Communication Training: Boosting Customer Experience. Recommended for Liam Carter."

5. Short and simple in a chat window

Tables show the bottom 3 first. "Show more" opens the full list, and "Show less" closes it. We also tried simple line graphs in the chat, but nothing complex. A chat window is small, and a chart there must be clear at a glance.

6. Ask for trust first, then earn it

Early answers carried one line: "Please validate the results for accuracy." In later phases, as accuracy improved and more answers came from RAG-based systems, we showed that line less.

7. Alerts that ask for a reply

When the scheduling tool (IEX) tags a team member as NCNS (no call, no show), Maestro sends an alert: "Hey! One of your direct reportee {Full Name} is tagged as NCNS for the shift today. Reach out and make sure everything is okay!" The team lead replies with one tap:

  • Acknowledge, contacted TM
  • Acknowledge, TM can not be contacted
  • Need Correction, connecting with WFM
  • Coach

The alert does not stop at a fact. It ends with an action.

8. Control over the noise

Every alert has a "Notification Configuration" chip. It opens the frequency settings in the chat. The team lead picks one: every 1 hour, every 2 hours, 1 hour before the shift ends, or 3 minutes before the shift ends. Maestro confirms the change in one line: "Frequency changed to '1 hour before the shift ends'."

9. Errors in plain words

When Maestro cannot answer, the card says so. It shows a clear "Error!" label, the question the team lead asked, and one line that names the problem. For example, a team lead asked for their campaign's GPR attainment (the share of a set target reached over a day, a week or a month). The card explains that the employee is not a direct report of that supervisor.

The later version: from an answer to a watch

The "UI including AI" page shows the next version of Maestro. This version was built too.

1. Insights written from the data

Maestro writes a short reading of the numbers: the overall result, the trend, who is at the top and the bottom, and what to do next. For example: team adherence was 95.4%, and the weekly trend moved from 95.1% in week 1 to 96.7% in week 2.

2. Want me to keep an eye on this?

After an answer, Maestro offers to watch the number: "Set up a periodic check and ping you if there are significant changes? For example: if your adherence drops by 3%." One question can become a standing alert. An on-demand answer becomes a proactive one.

3. Words that say exactly what happens

The first option set had two choices: "Notify me on significant changes" and "Notify me once a hour." In a call about the product's tone, with our senior development manager, project manager and senior director, I spoke up for the user. A user would read the hourly option two ways: a message every hour, or a message every hour only when something changes. Those are different things. So I added a third option, and each option now says exactly what it does:

  • Ping me as soon as it changes
  • Ping me hourly on significant changes
  • Send me an hourly summary

4. Ask about a file

A team lead can attach a spreadsheet and ask: "Analyse this document, who are my top performers?"

5. Policy answers in the chat

"What's the leave policy?" gets a short summary in a few points. The answer says whose policy it is, for example employees in India, and links the full document.

Prototypes for the room

I made one prototype for each flow, and I demoed both to clients, with VPs and directors in the room.

  • Proactive ("Auto-Delay"). The messages arrive in one go, as a scheduled summary or an alert would.
  • On demand ("On-Click"). Each step comes on a click, as a team lead asks a question and then a follow-up.

Both run inside the full Google Chat screen with TaskUs branding. Clients saw Maestro where their teams would use it.

Outcome

Maestro went live as a service, configured for many clients, so they could reach their business data in chat, on demand and proactively. Internal TaskUs OMs and client QAs used it to manage their campaigns. Maestro became TaskUs's own operational tool.

What I learned

Learn how people talk before you design how a product talks. I found almost nothing to copy, so I studied conversation itself. Our users were casual and short with words, so Maestro had to be too. Tone is a detail, and details decide if people keep using a product.

Design inside the real limits. I recreated Google Chat in Figma, so every screen showed what Google Chat could really hold. The limits shaped the design, and that is why every version could be built.

Speak for the user when they are not in the room. In a call about tone, the hourly option looked fine to everyone. I knew a user would read it two ways. One more option fixed it before any user was confused.
