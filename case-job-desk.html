Job Desk (Built with AI): four agents do the legwork, I make the calls

About 10 minutes to read

Job Desk is the tool I designed and built for my own job search in the UK. It is one web page with a shared database, and four AI agents read and write it. One agent finds and scores roles. One writes an application pack for each role I choose. One keeps my tracker true to my inbox. One reads the two job boards the others cannot reach, in my own browser.

The agents prepare. I decide. No agent can submit an application or send an email, and no agent runs until I press Run now. I designed and built it alone, with Claude as my engineering pair, and I kept changing it for five weeks as I used it. It is my clearest example of working as a design engineer: the product, the interface, the data model and the rules for each agent, all from one person.

The brief

In October 2026 I was looking for my next senior or lead product design role in the UK. The work was everywhere. Roles sat on LinkedIn, Welcome to the Jungle, Totaljobs, Wellfound and many company career pages. Replies came to two inboxes. My tracker was a spreadsheet with 127 rows, and my leads were in another sheet.

Each good application took the same steps. Read the listing. Find the real form behind the job board. Check the size of the company. Tailor the CV. Answer every question in my own words, with facts I can stand behind. Then remember to follow up ten days later.

None of this is hard. All of it takes time, and the time goes to admin instead of to the applications that matter. I wanted a tool that does the legwork and leaves every decision to me.

The limits I wrote first

Before I designed a screen, I wrote down what the agents must never do:

  • Never submit an application. They prepare. I press submit.
  • Never send an email. Gmail is read only: no sending, replying, drafting, labelling or deleting.
  • Never change a decision I made. A status I chose, a note I wrote, pack text I edited and the platform I applied through all stay as I left them.
  • Never guess a number. A team size needs a source. A claim about my work must come from my factual record.
  • Never stop to ask. When something is unclear, take the most reasonable reading, carry on, and write the assumption down.

These limits sit at the top of every agent's instructions. They also made the rest of the design simpler. When the agents can only prepare, the interface only has to help me decide.

How the desk fits together

Job Desk is one page with five tabs: Leads, Packs, Tracker, Insights and Agents. Behind it is one database with five parts: leads, packs, applications, the run log and the settings. The agents and I read and write the same data, so a change shows on my laptop and my phone at the same time.

Designing the desk

1. One row per job, wherever it is listed

The same role often shows on four or five sites, and many roles are posted again and again. Each lead has a key made from the company and the job title. So a repost updates the same row: last seen, times seen, and one more source link. The main button names the platform it opens, chosen by my preferred order. An "Also on" line lists the other places.

2. A score that explains itself

Every lead gets three scores from 1 to 5: skills, level and practicality. Fit is out of 10. Skills carry half the weight, and level and practicality a quarter each. Under the score, one line says why the role fits, tied to a named project of mine. One more line names the real risk. A confidence label says where the facts came from: High for the employer's own listing, Medium for a board summary, Low for a short snippet.

3. Small teams first, with evidence

I wanted small teams first. Small means about 200 people or fewer, a first, founding or only designer role, or a design team of five or fewer. Every team size comes with its source and the date of the check, because a guessed headcount is worse than none. A green tile at the top of Leads shows the open small-team roles that score 8 or more, with one tap to show only those.

4. Everything for one application in one place

A pack holds every question from the employer's real form, each with a drafted answer and the character limit, where the form has one. It also holds a cover letter, a LinkedIn note and a follow-up message, CV tweaks, evidence and interview prompts. Flags at the top list what the agent could not check, for example a form behind a sign-in. The pack list and the open pack scroll on their own. Edits save in place, and no agent writes over text I changed.

5. Know where replies come from

A job board is where a role is listed. A hiring system, such as Ashby or Greenhouse, is the software behind the employer's own form. They are different things, so the tracker records both. Each row shows the employer's system, with a "via" line for the board I applied through when it differs. Insights then shows, for each platform, how many applications I sent, how many got a reply, how many went to a next stage, and the typical time to a reply.

6. A way out, and a way back

Any job, in any status, can be marked Not interested or Expired, from its row or from its pack. Restore puts it back to the status it had before. When I mark an applied job Expired, its tracker row closes too. That happens only while the row is still open: waiting for a reply, or not yet submitted. Restore opens it again. Closed packs move to the bottom of the list and drop out of the counts. The tracker agent now leaves marked jobs alone, so a late email cannot undo my choice.

Designing the agents

1. Clear lines between the agents

Each agent owns its own work. Agent 1 reads job alerts, and Agent 3 reads replies to my applications, so neither reads the other's email. Only Agent 2 writes packs. Agents can reopen a job that an agent closed, but never one that I marked. When two agents could touch the same thing, the instructions say who wins.

2. Facts come from one record

The agents write in my name, so they must not invent. My profile in the database holds my CV text, a factual record that overrides the CV, my projects, my writing voice, standard answers and the people I already know. To change a fact, I change it in one place, not in four sets of instructions. Every agent also follows the same style rules: UK English, plain words, no em dashes and no emojis.

3. Every run shows its working

Each run writes one entry to the run log: its counts, what it did, and every assumption it made. A scout run notes which sources failed, which roles it dropped and why. An agent messages me only when something matters: a strong new lead, a next step from an employer, a follow-up that is due, or a problem. It is the same idea I used in EvaluateUs: show the AI's working, so a person can check it.

4. A browser agent with good manners

Two boards were closed to the cloud agents. Welcome to the Jungle shows jobs only as matches to a signed-in user, and Totaljobs blocks automated readers. So Agent 4 reads them in my own Chrome. On the first check, job pages opened too fast, and Totaljobs blocked my browser for a while. So I wrote strict rules. At most six result pages a run, 15 seconds apart. Never a job page. Never a script that calls the site. On Welcome to the Jungle it reads only the matches list, because opening a job marks the match as seen. It never signs in, clicks Apply or changes a setting.

5. I set the pace

At first the agents ran on a schedule: the scout twice a week, packs every morning, the inbox sync every evening and the board check on weekdays. I wanted them to work when I work on jobs, not on a timer. So since 27 October, every agent runs only when I press Run now. Each agent starts from its own last run, with a small overlap, so a long gap misses nothing. After one gap of 19 days, the board check simply read the whole window.

What broke, and what I changed

Real use found the weak spots quickly.

  • Sources fail. In the first scout run, one job data source failed on every call. Later, the page reader hit its rate limit and once ran out of credits halfway through a run. Now an agent tries a failing source once, uses the next source, and says so in the log.
  • Runs overlap. Twice, two runs of the same agent worked at the same time. Every write carries the version it read, so a run cannot overwrite a newer change. A run that finds the work already done writes nothing and says why.
  • One agent cannot start another. Agent 4 is meant to start Agent 2 when it queues a strong lead, but a permission check stops that. I left it as it is. Queued leads wait for my next Run now, so nothing starts without me.

How I built it

I designed Job Desk and built it with Claude, in conversation. I set the product: the limits, who does what, the data model, the statuses and how each screen behaves. Claude wrote the page code and the agent instructions to that brief. I tested each version with real applications, found what was wrong, and asked for the next change.

The stack is small on purpose:

  • The page: one HTML file published as a Claude artifact, with a database that the page and the agents share.
  • The agents: four scheduled tasks in Claude. Each has written instructions, the tools it may use, and a Run now button on the Agents tab.
  • The connections: Gmail (read only for the agents), Firecrawl to read web pages, a job listings source, and Claude in Chrome for Agent 4.
  • No server and no deploy pipeline. A new version goes live when it is published, on desktop and on my phone.

Writing an agent's instructions turned out to be interaction design. Each set says what to read, what to write, what to leave alone, when to speak and how to report. The clearer the brief, the less the agent has to guess.

Outcome

On 26 November 2026, Job Desk held:

  • 195 roles, found, scored and kept in one list, each with a reason, a risk and a source.
  • More than 70 application packs, each written from the employer's real listing, and from the form itself wherever it could be read.
  • 58 applications sent from Job Desk leads. I pressed submit on each one myself. No agent can.
  • 44 logged agent runs, each with its counts and its assumptions.
  • One tracker for the 127 older applications and every new one, kept true to my inbox.

A job search session now starts on one page. I press Run now, read the new leads, open a pack, apply, and mark what is done.

Other things I built alone

Job Desk is not a one-off.

  • Job App Assistant. A live AI web app that I designed, coded and deployed alone in one session, with Node.js and the Claude API. It taught me where the back room of an AI product is. Job Desk goes further: four agents, a shared database, and a page that changes as I use it.
  • This website. I build and ship dipeshgurav.com myself. I brief Claude Code in VS Code, check each change on a Vercel preview, and merge it when it is right.

What I learned

Design the limits before the features. The first thing I wrote was what the agents must never do. Those limits made every screen simpler, because the desk only has to help me decide. When I can undo every choice, and no agent can undo it for me, I trust the agents with more of the legwork.

An agent's instructions are a design file. They decide what the agent reads, what it changes, what it leaves alone and when it speaks. A vague line in the instructions becomes a vague result on the screen. So I write them with the same care as a user flow.

Being the user is the fastest research. Every change in the table above started on a day when I needed something while I applied for a real job. I felt each gap the day it appeared, and the fix often went live the same day.
