TaskGPT case study: the AI that works inside the call

About 35 minutes to read

Part 1 TaskGPT, the whole suite

The AI that works inside the call

TaskGPT is the AI suite I designed at TaskUs for agents on live customer calls, and for the people who set up, measure and improve the AI behind them. I was the only designer, from research to developer handover. All 11 products and 7 Chrome extensions shipped and went live, across 14 countries, as part of a TaskUs product suite used by around 35,000 people a day. Call-handling time fell 20%, and operating costs fell 20%. The first TaskGPT chatbot, later named AssistAI, drove the first, fast drop, and AuthorAI and the extensions added to it.

Much of it ran as a Chrome extension inside the tool agents already used, so help arrived in the same window as the conversation. Seven Chrome extensions took TaskGPT into the tools teams already used: the browser, Zendesk and Kustomer.

The problem and the people

My first job was in a call centre, so I know what a mid-call tab switch costs: a pause the customer hears. Before TaskGPT, agents did this work by hand. They kept several portals, sheets and documents open, and searched them during the call for the right answer.

TaskUs serves many Fortune 500 tech companies, and each one asks for something different. Every client has its own product, its own after-sales service, and its own rules for refunds and returns. Each one also has its own picture of the solution. One multi-tenant product had to serve all of them.

The business watched three things:

  • Average handling time (AHT). Time on calls ties directly to the business TaskUs does with each client.
  • Quality and accuracy. How clearly the agent explained the solution.
  • Upsell and savings. Where agents could sell more, and where they could save the client money.

Most requests came from campaign managers inside TaskUs, on behalf of their clients. The next section shows how a request became a product.

How a request became a product

I was the only designer in TaskUs Digital IT, a team of about 35 people. Our direct clients were the campaign managers inside TaskUs. Each of them runs teams for an outside client, a "logo" in TaskUs terms, such as Meta.

  1. A logo needs something in content moderation, customer handling or other operations, and tells its campaign manager.
  2. If IT can automate the work, secure it or make it faster, the campaign manager takes it to our SVP or directors.
  3. In the first calls, the director, the managers and I write the business requirement document (BRD), with our first ideas and a timeline.
  4. I research the stakeholders, competitors, the market, existing solutions, and what we can build.
  5. I turn the research into personas and high-fidelity wireframes on the TaskGPT design system, then a prototype.
  6. Usability tests shape the MVP.
  7. After launch, live users send regular feedback, and we plan the improvements into sprints.

Requirements could change overnight, and now and then the whole scope changed. I worked so that a late change did not stall the release.

How I researched

I sat with agents through live calls, on the floor or over Zoom, to see how they used their systems and what would make their day easier. I listened in on calls where people described their problems, and sometimes their own idea of a fix. I met everyone in the chain: agents, operations managers, approvers, managers, and developers to close the technical gaps. The product and business requirement documents (PRDs and BRDs) gave me the business side.

Then I built early prototypes to test my ideas before anything went to development.

From one chatbot to a suite

Buddy-sitting showed me the real problem: finding the right information at the right time. That became the first TaskGPT, a chatbot set up for each client. It put the client's business information at the agent's fingertips, so agents no longer kept several portals open before a call. They could ask it anything about the client, the way they ask ChatGPT, and it answered from the client's own knowledge base through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). AHT dropped quickly.

Then it spread. Managers, sales teams and team leads started to use it, and soon every campaign wanted TaskGPT. It grew into campaigns in 14 countries, as part of a TaskUs suite used by around 35,000 people a day. When TaskGPT became the name of the whole suite, the chatbot took the name AssistAI.

Later, TaskUs moved its operations away from Google products and built its own browser. The work that lived in sheets and documents moved into TaskGPT. That is where Document Search comes in.

When TaskUs planned to sell the products commercially, the brand team caught up with us. We revamped the products a little and renamed some of them, which is how TaskScribe became AuthorAI. After that revamp, adoption rose by an average of 12%.

One real moment: the quarter-end demo

TaskUs is built on trust. It calls itself a people-first company, and "People First" sits on every TaskGPT login screen. I worked in more than one call centre during college, and none came close to TaskUs on culture, pay or work-life balance. The same trust runs through its client relationships. When TaskUs says a change will lift a client's content moderation or customer handling, the client believes it. That trust rests on transparency, and with AI, transparency means showing the numbers.

We moved fast from day one. Within a week of OpenAI's ChatGPT launch (opens in a new tab) on 30 November 2022, we demoed our own AI chatbot to a client. It put accurate answers at the agent's fingertips, for internal operations and for customer support. TaskGPT sold quickly. Developers onboarded client after client with Chatbotify, which I designed so that a new instance took minutes, while each client could still have its own customisations.

Near the end of a quarter, our SVP came to me with a question: how do we show clients, in simple numbers and visuals, how TaskGPT helps them? My manager wanted to go as deep as possible. The development manager wanted to show what happens in the code. I answered both with one design: simple on the surface, deep underneath.

I designed for one moment: our SVP on a client call, showing how TaskUs helps the client's teams deliver their best work without stress. So the first screen shows only the primary numbers: active users against provisioned users for each campaign, with a trend line. No wall of graphs and charts.

The depth sits one level down:

  • Every query. What each teammate asked, the response, whether they liked it, and whether it got an answer or failed.
  • Where it failed. A query passes through 16 steps before the answer arrives: from sensitive-information detection, masking and small talk, through input guardrails, the embedding call, the vector database and file reading, to the LLM call and output guardrails. A compact table opens inside the row, so people see every step without changing screens or going back and forth.
  • The full trail. The conversation between the system and the AI, with its reasoning. The citations behind each answer, which show the knowledge base it came from, or reveal that the model made one up. Metadata and LLM details in their own tabs.

My whole focus was to make that call a success. The next quarter, TaskGPT sold even faster, and existing clients asked which other TaskGPT tools could help their customer support and their internal operations.

What I instrumented. Eight groups of KPIs, each with a one-line definition in the product.

The extensions carry the same idea. Each Chrome extension opens on the person's own numbers: queries this session, characters translated, or time on task. The people who use the AI see its effect, not only their managers.

Five rules I used across all three

  1. Every number has its definition next to it. A number with no definition starts an argument. Each KPI card has an info icon with one line, such as "Active users count out of total provisioned users on the system".
  2. Ratios, not raw counts. Active users show against provisioned users, and valid responses against all responses. A share tells a manager what to do. A count alone does not.
  3. One filter grammar. Product, instance, campaign, LOB, site and date range work the same way in the dashboard, Observability and Reports. People learn it once.
  4. From a number to the rows behind it. Each KPI card opens its detail. Knowledge gaps, guardrail hits, valid queries and small talk show as lists of real queries with usage counts.
  5. Show where it broke. In Observability, each step of the AI pipeline has a letter, so a person can name the failed step in a ticket.

How it grew. Reports start from one form: pick a product, an instance, a report type and a date range, then download it or view it. View Report opens the rows on screen, with the same filters as the dashboard. The report types grew to cover audit logs, adoption and feedback, and daily active users. The June 2024 Observability update gave each interaction a detailed view with a review step. In March 2025 the dashboard gained a review summary: how many interactions, knowledge gaps and thumbs-down answers were reviewed, and why.

The numbers lead to a fix. A reviewer marks an answer as not acceptable and picks the reason: knowledge gap, guardrails or knowledge base. Knowledge gaps go to the knowledge owners. They fix the article in the KnowledgeBase Utility and publish a new version, and AssistAI shows that version in the chat. If a dashboard does not help someone act, it is just a beautiful representation of your data.

Trust and safety by design

Agents will not use an AI on a live call if they cannot trust it with customer data. So I put safety into the screens, not only into the code.

  • PII masking. Each chatbot has masking rules. Numbers such as ATM card, Aadhaar, PAN and driving licence numbers change to stars before the text goes to the model. Master rules apply to every chatbot.
  • Sensitive-information check. If an agent types something that looks sensitive, the chat asks first: Don't share, Share masked, or Yes (not recommended). The safe choice comes first.
  • Guardrails in and out. Incoming and outgoing guardrail hits show on the dashboard and on each logged query.
  • A privacy line under every input. It tells people not to share passwords or financial data, and that the AI can make mistakes.
  • Chat visibility. A chat is private, shared with the supervisor and immediate reporter (the default), or public. A change of chat type tells the supervisor.
  • A backup engine. BCP moves every chatbot to a second AI provider in one action.
  • Roles and privileges. Each role sees only what it needs (table below).
  • An encrypted layer. Moving the suite behind an encrypted layer solved the privacy problem.
  • Moderator wellbeing. Shield blurs images before a moderator sees them and reminds them to take breaks. Shield Co-Pilot explains each policy, so moderators do not have to search for it.
  • A score on every guess. TranslateBuddy and Deepfake Detection show a confidence score, and TranslateBuddy warns when accuracy drops.

Design system and how I worked

TaskGPT used the TaskUs design system, with its own component layer on top. The global components could not cover TaskGPT's multi-tenant admin. There, a team onboarded clients, imported employees, and set the tools, AI model and markdown knowledge base for each campaign. I agreed the layer with developers and managers, and planned to merge it back as a branch for reuse.

What the Figma file shows about the process:

  • Components for every state. Date range presets, single and multi-select dropdowns, input states, table row states, tooltips, badges, and error, success and system modals.
  • An admin for every product. All products were multi-tenant, so each one had its own admin, designed for control of user roles.
  • Accessibility. I designed the suite and the extensions to WCAG 2.2 AA.
  • Versions kept side by side. Document Search is on version 4 and TranslateBuddy on version 3. Agentic AI, TaskScribe, Shield and Productivity Tracker each have an old and a new design. Older versions sit on their own pages.
  • A framework page per product. Most hold the older versions of the product. Some also hold the process, and the thoughts I noted while I built it.
  • Research beside the work. The extension files keep screenshots of the earlier screens and of reference designs next to the new work.
  • Specs with defaults. The TaskScribe file defines each writing feature, what it is for, and its default setting.
  • Feedback on the live build. Live users sent regular usability feedback, and we planned it into sprints. The AuthorAI feedback section (September 2024) puts screenshots of the build beside the fixes. Notes on the TaskGPT extension, such as "Reduce the height of the button by 20%", track small fixes.
  • Notes for developers. Click-by-click annotations in the KnowledgeBase Utility, and a dashboard "updated as per development requirement" in March 2025.
  • The business's own words. When AI suggested interface labels, I kept only the ones that matched the words agents and clients already used.

Outcome and what I learned

All 11 products and 7 extensions shipped and went live. TaskGPT ran across 14 countries as part of a TaskUs product suite used by around 35,000 people a day. The TaskGPT chatbot, later named AssistAI, gave the first, fast drop: once agents had each client's details at their fingertips, AHT fell, and efficiency and accuracy rose. AuthorAI and the extensions added to it, on calls and in content moderation. Together they cut call-handling time by 20% and operating costs by 20%, and we reviewed the results with clients on calls. Setting up a new client instance went from about a day of code to minutes in Chatbotify, and after the commercial revamp, adoption rose by an average of 12%. Because measurement was part of the design, the team could see adoption, answer quality and token cost side by side, and decide where to invest next.

  1. Put help where the work is. A second app in the middle of a call is a pause the customer hears.
  2. Show what the AI did. The knowledge base version, the source, the BCP banner and the status flag let people see the system's state. Trust follows.
  3. Design for the next decision, not the data. Each dashboard view answers one person's next question.
  4. Close the loop. A knowledge gap is only useful when it reaches the person who can fix it.
  5. Let real use shape the next feature. The AuthorAI templates came straight from a survey of how people used TaskGPT.
  6. Propose, don't wait. I pitched the Home Page and the dashboard myself, and both went into development.

Part 2 Product by product

01 Document Search

Ask one question across a client's whole document library, and get an answer for each file with its reasoning and citation.

The story. It began as a proof of concept for the sales team, and went live after the proof of concept worked. A lead who manages several top logos came to Digital IT with a problem. From cold lead to warm lead to signed client, the same questions come up in meeting after meeting. Everyone involved in onboarding has to answer them again, from more than 50 long documents, with KPI data and citations. Each client call also needed the people who knew those details on the call.

They did not bring a solution, only how they worked. I joined a call with my managers to see it. They used several sheets and the find function to hunt for a term across many documents, in as few tries as they could. I also saw that they kept the files in Google Drive, so I proposed a Google Drive connector.

What I designed

  • One question field. People pick a saved question or write a new one, then choose the clients to search. Version 4 adds a list of processed clients and quick chips to add a new client.
  • Results for each file. Client, path link, answer, reasoning and citation, plus a count such as "45/45 file(s) found matching your query". Results export.
  • A queue state. "Your question is in the queue, it will be processed soon", with skeleton cards, because long searches run in the background.
  • Requested Questions. Every question with who asked it, its status (success, in progress with "4/5 files", failed or terminated) and a Terminate action for long runs.
  • Manage document search, for admins. Pick the client and the connector (Google Drive), paste and verify a path link, include sub-folders, and sync now. A Files tab shows the sync status of each file. A Questions tab holds the saved questions.

Design decisions

  • Start from intent. Under the hood the product can do a lot, but people come here to ask a question. So the page opens on one field: a saved question or a new one.
  • The least clicks possible. Pick the clients, then search. The Google Drive connector syncs the files of many clients at once.
  • No waiting. A search goes into a queue, and people carry on with other work while the agent finds the answers.
  • Keep every answer. Answers, reasoning and citations are stored, and people view, share or download them in one click.
  • Check before use. Answer, reasoning and citation sit together, so a person can check the AI before they use the answer.

Later phases planned more analytics and a bulk way to upload questions: upload an Excel file from a template, check its format, show results as they process, and download them.

02 Home Page

One start page for every TaskGPT product, and for every client instance a person can open.

The story. I pitched the Home Page to my manager. Every TaskGPT tool would open from one place, and the same page would serve admins: themes, BCP, roles, global and master settings, and user settings. I wanted it to feel like a front-end product, not an admin control panel. After his yes, I designed it with ideas from SaaS products used worldwide. The team liked how it showed every product they had built, and it went to development soon after. We later built the mobile version. An instance is a copy of a product set up for one client, for example AuthorAI for Adidas. The recent list is the admin view: it shows the instances created for clients, so admins can manage them and see how they perform.

What I designed

  • Product cards for every TaskGPT product, with Products, Analytics and Observability tabs.
  • An Instances table. Name, last used, product, and a link that opens the instance. Filter chips for each product, a search, and a Recent view with its own empty state: "You have no recently used product instances".
  • A settings panel. Activate BCP; manage users, roles, AssistAI and AuthorAI; global and master configuration; change or reset role; change time zone; log out.
  • A mobile layout (built after the desktop version).

Design decisions

  • The main list is instances, because admins manage and track each client's copy of a product.
  • BCP sits on the home page, so an admin can act in one step during an outage.
  • Role switch, managed by admins. Managers and admins use it to see the teammate view, check how a campaign performs, debug a problem, or prepare for a client call.

03 Reports

Records of what happened in TaskGPT, for people who need rows, not charts.

The story. By then the suite had about 8 products, serving hundreds of clients and thousands of lines of business, run by more than 10,000 people across 14+ sites. Everyone asked how the product was doing, each for a different reason. Some had to justify the cost, and some the results. Some asked about adoption, others about engagement. I worked it out with our SVP, the director and the development manager. Reports answer all of them: audit logs, feedback on how agents perform, and daily and monthly active users (DAU and MAU).

What I designed

  • The report form. Pick the product (for example Knowledge Assist), the instance, the report type and the date range. Then download, or view.
  • The report view. View Report opens the rows on screen, with the same filters as the dashboard: campaign, LOB and site chips, a date range, rows per page and a download button. The header says which report and which instance you see.
  • Report types: conversation history logs, audit logs, AssistAI adoption and feedback, and daily active users. The adoption report states its scope: teammate and SME data only.
  • Product feedback report. Average product, quality and NLU ratings, each with its spread of star ratings, and the written feedback beside them, with sort and filters.
  • Components for each state: date presets (today, yesterday, last week, last month, custom), single and multi-select dropdowns, table row states, tooltips, and success and error modals.

Design decisions

  • View before download. The form offers both, so people check the rows on screen and download only what they need.
  • One filter grammar across reports and dashboards, so a manager learns it once.
  • Each report states whose data it holds.

04 AuthorAI (TaskScribe)

A writing assistant for agents and teams: draft, rewrite and summarise in the right tone. Its earlier name was TaskScribe.

The story. We wanted to go one step beyond chat. Writing a good prompt takes time, so we built one-click features on top of it. After TaskGPT launched, I ran a survey on how it helped agents and everyone else who used it. The patterns were clear:

  • Managers mostly summarised and drafted emails.
  • Team leads checked sentiment in conversations, and pulled action items from emails and documents.
  • Agents improved their writing and grammar, and polished their tone.

From these patterns I planned one-click templates: summarise (in bullets or paragraphs), sentiment analysis, change tone, improve writing, and drafts of emails, articles and announcements. Admins control access and see usage, so the features keep growing. The product got so much praise that we launched it on its own. When TaskUs planned to sell it commercially, the brand team named it AuthorAI (it started as TaskScribe). After that revamp, adoption across the renamed products rose by an average of 12%.

What I designed

  • A shortcut menu in the prompt bar. Write an email, a message, an article or a sentence; create an announcement; paraphrase; rephrase; templates; write a custom prompt.
  • Actions on text. Summarise, find action items, improve writing, fix spelling and grammar, change tone (professional, friendly or casual), sentiment analysis, and a pronunciation helper for one word.
  • Output layouts. Paragraph, bullet list or table.
  • Favourite templates, so common tasks take one click.
  • A privacy caution under the input. Do not share passwords or financial data, and the AI can make mistakes.
  • Article Generation. Upload a DOC, TXT or PDF (up to 10 MB), or a URL, and get an article back. A list shows each document as processing, finished or failed, with download and retry. Errors say what to do, for example "File size too big, maximum allowed 10mb" with Re-Upload.
  • A threaded view that puts AuthorAI content inside an AssistAI chat, plus light and dark themes.

Design decisions

  • Shortcuts start from the task, such as "write an email", not from a blank prompt, because writing a good prompt takes time.
  • The templates come from real use. Each one matches a pattern from the survey.
  • The September 2024 feedback round sits in the file, with screenshots of the live build beside the fixes.

AuthorAI also ran as a Chrome extension. Its floating widget works on almost any website or tool the agent uses, as long as the extension is on. It also has its own panel in the Kustomer agent workspace. Section 12 covers both.

05 Observability

See every AI interaction step by step, and judge if the answer was acceptable.

The story. Our SVP proposed it, after the quarter-end question that shaped the design (see "One real moment" in Part 1). It became TaskUs's first actionable instrumentation of AI tools on client campaigns, built on two principles: data minimisation and privacy by design. The idea was to follow each query through every step: sensitive-information detection and masking, small talk, input guardrails, file reading, the vector database, the model call and output guardrails, until the answer reaches the user. For each step it records the time taken, and if a step fails, where and why. People can debug down to the raw data from the front end. My job was to make heavy, complex data easy to read, understand and fix.

What I designed

  • Campaign view. Total active users and total provisioned users this month, each with its change and a definition you can open ("What is Active Users?"). A table of campaigns shows active users, provisioned users and an active-user trend line. Export to CSV.
  • Interaction logs. One row per query: user, time, query, feedback (thumbs up or down), the response, and a status flag.
  • The status flag. A bar of lettered steps for the AI pipeline: chatbot triggered, incoming guardrails, embedding call, vector database call, sent to the model, outgoing guardrails, PII masking and request status. The letter shows where a request stopped. Hover over it to see the overall time, the step that failed, and how the time splits between the middleware and the AI. Open the row to see each step with its timestamp, time taken and a remark such as "Request failed".
  • Interaction detail. Tabs for response, prompt, metadata, LLM, feedback and citation. Metadata shows the masking flag, response time, database and model times, and tokens. LLM shows the engine, model, embedding model and BCP mode. Citation shows the file, document ID, version, chunk and score.
  • Review. "Is the response acceptable?" Yes or No. A No asks for the reason: knowledge gap, guardrails or knowledge base. The view shows who reviewed it last, and Previous and Next move through the queue. Log review also shows the original text beside a translation.
  • Smaller layouts at 1280 px wide for review on laptops.

Design decisions

  • Each step has a letter, so a person can name the failed step in a ticket. Colour alone cannot do that.
  • Complex data in tabs: response, prompt, metadata, LLM, feedback and citation. Each person opens only what they need.
  • The three review reasons match the review chart on the dashboard, so every review counts in the numbers.
  • After a fix in the back end, the reviewer runs the query again from the prompt tab to check it.

06 AssistAI

The knowledge assistant agents use on live calls. It answers from the client's own knowledge base.

The story. AssistAI is the first TaskGPT chatbot, the one at the start of this case study. When TaskGPT became the name of the whole suite, the chatbot became AssistAI. It is the fully equipped chatbot agents use on calls: each client's details at their fingertips, answered through RAG from that client's own knowledge base.

What I designed

  • A chat that shows its source of truth. The header shows the client and the knowledge base version, for example "Ver. 15". When the knowledge base changes during a chat, a divider says so: "KB updated to Ver. 15.0.0".
  • Answer tools. A source tag (for example "Source: Web"), related questions, stats on demand (tokens and metadata), clear context, copy, regenerate, and thumbs up or down.
  • A playground switch, to test a bot before release.
  • Chat history with three visibility levels: private, default (you, your supervisor and your immediate reporter) and public. A change of chat type asks for confirmation, and says that the supervisor gets a notification.
  • Feedback in two layers. On each response (positive or negative, with a reason). On the product (an emoji rating, "Did you get the answers for your questions?", and which product they needed).
  • A floating version. A compact panel with a chatbot picker, a drawer for chat history, and file upload (the Chrome extensions are in section 12).
  • A sensitive-information check before a message goes out. The chat warns that the message looks like sensitive information, and offers three choices: Don't share, Share masked, or Yes (not recommended).
  • A welcome screen that sets expectations. It remembers the conversation, takes corrections and declines inappropriate requests. Error states for when the service is down. Light, dark and mobile layouts.

Design decisions

  • Show the version. Agents and reviewers can tie an answer to the knowledge it came from.
  • In the sensitive-information check, the safe choice comes first, and the risky choice says "Not recommended".
  • Help goes where the agent already is. A mid-call tab switch is a pause the customer hears.

07 Agentic AI

Build AI agents that do work with tools, not only answer questions. I designed it in early 2025, and it went live.

The story. Our SVP proposed a system where anyone can create their own agent. We were among the first to try, and we were all learning. I simplified it by giving people every brick an agent needs to run. They start from ready-made instances built on real use cases, so they can build fast and simply, with no loss of capability. The builder holds every setting an agent needs, step by step, so nobody is overwhelmed.

What I designed

  • A framework first. Six layers: models, tools, knowledge and memory, audio and speech, guardrails, and orchestration. Each layer became a part of the builder.
  • Version 1, inside the chatbot setup. LLMs, tools and environment; add or edit internal and external tools and their parameters; a feedback switch; and how many days to keep logs.
  • The new AgenticAI app. Dashboard, Agents and Connectors, plus functions, web search, file search, computer use, a form builder and email templates.
  • The agent builder. Name; system instructions (role and agent instruction); model (Amazon Bedrock, OpenAI or PaLM 2); tools (file search, code interpretation, functions); connectors (ServiceNow, Zendesk, Power BI, Kustomer, Jira, Workday, Slack); and the response format.
  • A live preview beside the form. It shows the agent's chain of thought: what it searches (knowledge base, TaskGPT database, web) and which tools it reads (for example Power BI, Workday and ServiceNow). Logs are one click away.
  • A flow canvas for guided conversations: a start node, messages with options, branches, and Create Ticket nodes with a tag, a model and a prompt. Save as a draft, then Launch.

Design decisions

  • Start from a ready-made instance, not a blank page.
  • One step at a time. Every setting an agent needs is there, without overwhelming the person.
  • Show the thinking. When an agent acts on business systems, people need to see what it read before they trust what it did.
  • Form and preview side by side, so every change shows its effect at once.

08 Chatbotify

Create and manage a chatbot (a Knowledge Assist) for each client, with no code.

Chatbotify was the configuration layer before Agentic AI. Clients used it to manage their chatbots, and admins used it to create chatbots for clients. Agentic AI grew out of it. The first instance was built entirely in code, which took about a day. I designed Chatbotify so that a new client instance took minutes, with room for each client's own customisations.

What I designed

  • A stepped setup: General, Knowledge Base and App Integrations, with Basic and Advanced tabs.
  • Knowledge base setup. Choose a version or add a new one. Single or bulk upload. Create a new repository or update one. Four sources: local files, Google Drive, Glowstick API or an external API. Then the embedding model, the extraction method and the chunk method.
  • "Info about this KB". Upload time, total cost, tokens used, total chunks and the vector index, so the cost of knowledge shows at setup.
  • An upload tracker in five steps: file processing, text extraction, embeddings, vector database and knowledge repository.
  • Structure checks. Each file shows if it has markdown headings. A warning asks for at least 3 headers or 200 lines. A PDF gets a warning to use DOC or TXT, because the system cannot read headings in a PDF. "More info" counts the headings at each level.
  • Advanced settings. The provider and language model (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM 2, Llama 2, Jurassic-2, Titan and Claude 2) and the embedding model; small talk questions and answers; PII masking rules; playground instructions; welcome message; promote; app integrations.
  • Master configuration for rules that apply to every chatbot: master small talk, master masking, and BCP for AI.

Design decisions

  • Tell people why a file will fail before it fails. Files without headings gave conflicting or missing answers, so the upload checks structure first.
  • Two levels of rules. TaskUs admins create master rules, such as masking and small talk, once. Each client then switches them on or off for its own chatbot.

09 BCP (Business Continuity Plan)

One action moves every chatbot from the primary AI engine to a backup engine when the primary fails.

The story. BCP was built for days like the one when OpenAI had an outage. A lot of business was affected, and teams everywhere had to switch their AI models by hand. A client also asked us for a fallback. So we planned fallback settings for all our client instances: they choose the AI provider, the LLM model and its version for the primary and secondary engines. With BCP on, the secondary model answers instead. I planned it with my managers and designed it.

What I designed

  • A BCP screen in master configuration. Admins choose the AI provider, the LLM model and its version for each engine: primary (for example OpenAI) and secondary (for example Anthropic). One button activates it.
  • A confirmation step before the switch, with Confirm and Not now.
  • A clear active state. A red banner says BCP is active and the secondary AI engine now runs, "excluding Exceptions". "Exceptions" opens the client's own exceptions document in a new browser tab, for provisioned users. Deactivate reverses it, with its own confirmation.
  • A banner on every screen. While BCP is on, every screen of every product shows it. Agent chats also show a BCP badge and "Powered by Anthropic", so agents know why answers may change.
  • BCP in other places: a toggle in the home settings panel, and a BCP mode field in the LLM details of each logged interaction.

Design decisions

  • One action, with a confirmation. An outage is stressful, so the switch is simple but hard to press by mistake.
  • Say it everywhere it matters: in admin, in the agent chat and in the logs.
  • Exceptions are written per client. Each client has its own exceptions document: the cases the backup engine does not cover, and how agents should handle them. It sits one click away from the banner.

The backup engine was also a selling point. In the TaskGPT extension, the subscription upgrade lists a "Back-Up AI Engine for Emergency (No Down Time)" next to more knowledge bases and control of user access.

10 KnowledgeBase Utility

A content tool where client content managers and operations managers write, version and publish the answers the chatbots use.

The story. I worked it out with our SVP, the senior director and the developer lead, to help clients build better knowledge bases for their AI tools. Knowledge sits in several layers and keeps changing. So I proposed a branch model, like GitHub, to manage versions and to add or remove context. The articles come from clients, and their content managers and operations managers keep them up to date. Our tools help them format the articles and create the markdown the chatbots need.

What I designed

  • Repositories. Name, active version, created by, last updated and status, with filters by date and status.
  • Versions in three states: published, draft and archived. A "Latest" badge marks the version the chatbot uses.
  • Articles inside each version. A question title, an intent category, tags, a rich-text response, and an optional internal response: "a followup question or suggestion for the teammate".
  • Two ways to edit: side by side with the article list, or full screen.
  • Actions: publish, move to draft, archive (with a warning that archiving removes the version from the Knowledge Assist), export to file and copy link.
  • Annotations for developers: what each link opens, and where warnings appear.

Design decisions

  • Articles are questions and answers, because the chatbot answers questions.
  • The internal response is for the agent only. The customer gets the answer, and the agent gets a next step.
  • Versions work like branches in GitHub, not edits in place. Every change stays traceable, and a reviewer can tie an answer to a version.

11 TaskGPT Dashboard

One page that tells a manager if the AI is used, if it is right, and what it costs.

The story. Like the Home Page, I proposed it, and everyone liked it. This is the screen where client trust formed, and the screen that sold TaskGPT most. It grew with the suite, as each new product added its numbers. The overview tracks users, sessions, responses and consumption. The feedback view tracks feature adoption, and averages each query's ratings for overall satisfaction, quality and coherence, and natural language understanding (NLU), with thumbs-up and thumbs-down details.

What I designed

  • Scope first. Product and instance at the top. Then filter chips for campaign, LOB and site, a designation filter (teammate, team lead, manager, operations manager) and a timeframe (today, yesterday, last 7 days, month to date, year to date or custom).
  • Six KPI groups in columns: users, engagement, responses, guardrails, sessions and consumption. Each card shows a ratio and a percentage, a trend line, and an arrow to the detail.
  • A definition for each KPI behind an info icon that shows on hover. For example: "Average tokens consumed per user in selected timeframe".
  • Tables under the KPIs: knowledge-gap queries, guardrail queries (input or output), valid queries and small-talk queries, each with a usage count.
  • A Feedbacks tab, and a review dashboard (March 2025). Feedback submitted, knowledge gaps, and thumbs up and down. Interactions, knowledge gaps and thumbs-down answers reviewed. A review disposition chart by reason (knowledge gap, guardrails, knowledge base and more).
  • Download and email the dashboard; empty and loading states; layouts at 1024 px and 1440 px wide.

Design decisions

  • Ratios over raw counts. A share of provisioned users tells a manager what to do.
  • Consumption sits beside quality. Token cost per user and per session shows where the oil is going.
  • Knowledge gaps show as a list of real queries, not only a number, so a knowledge owner can fix them in the KnowledgeBase Utility.

Shield

Content moderators see harmful images and videos all day. Shield is a wellbeing extension, "brought to you by your Wellness & Resiliency Department". It started as a developer build that the marketing team demoed to a client who asked for it. The client liked what it could do, but the interface looked old and below standard. So it came to me. The developer build packed every tool into rows of tab buttons on one cramped screen. I turned it into a home of six cards, each with an icon and an on or off state, and gave each tool its own screen with a short explanation.

The tools: image blur before the moderator sees anything (intensity, partial blur, greyscale, reveal on hover), so they can prepare and take a breath; reminders to stretch, drink water and breathe; Clear and Restore, a guided exercise that replaces a disturbing image in the mind with a calm one; Sensation Cleanse, an eye-movement exercise to calm the body; and Spatial Games and a Sound Studio for breaks.

Shield Co-Pilot

An AI co-pilot inside the moderation tool. It scans text or images, on its own or on request, and highlights the words that break a policy. A side panel lists the detected policies and sub-policies by severity (critical, high, medium, low). It defines each one, explains why the content is a violation, and links to the client's policy article. It detects and translates other languages. Moderators no longer switch screens to look up a policy, which saves time on every review.

Design decisions

  • Go to the work. Each extension sits beside the page the person already uses, so nobody changes tabs in the middle of a ticket.
  • Protect the people who see the worst content. Shield lets moderators choose when to look. Co-Pilot explains the policy, so they do not have to search for it.
  • Show confidence. Translation and deepfake results carry a score, so people know how far to trust them.
  • Ask the person to confirm. Co-Pilot, TranslateBuddy and Deepfake Detection all ask for a rating or a check, and that feedback improves the AI.
