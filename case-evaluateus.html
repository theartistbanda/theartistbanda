EvaluateUs: from typed questions to a live AI customer

About 11 minutes to read

EvaluateUs is an AI assessment platform I designed at TaskUs. Teams use it to hire, coach and train agents. An AI scores every answer, and three people read that result: the candidate, the assessor who checks the score, and the manager who decides what happens next.

FactDetail
RoleLead Product Designer and the only designer: research, planning, design, prototypes, handover, and UI testing of the build
TeamA senior director of development, a senior development manager, developers, testers and me
TimelineFirst version went live in December 2025. AI scenario version: September 2025 to March 2026
PlatformWeb: an admin and manager console, and a candidate assessment page

The brief

TaskUs hires and trains agents for many client campaigns. Before an agent talks to a customer, the campaign needs to know one thing: can this person communicate well? A language check was one round of the interview. The same checks came back in coaching and training.

The data behind those checks lived in fragments: several Google Sheets, Boost for coaching, Workday for one-to-ones, and teammate lists sent around by email. Team leads filtered one sheet to decide whom to coach, and tracked coaching history in another. Nobody could see how hiring and training were going in one place.

The PRD set the business goals: spend less time and effort on manual evaluation, and make scores fairer and more accurate.

Planning: what the product had to do

I started with a plain list of what the product had to do, and grew it into a PRD. The list covered:

  • Language evaluations for an interview round, shared through a secret link or a passcode.
  • Settings for each evaluation: number of attempts, pass marks, anti-cheating rules, fixed or random question order, and scoring by the system or by a person.
  • A weight for each skill: grammar, sentence construction, tenses, punctuation and spelling.
  • Questions written in the product, or uploaded as a document.
  • Automatic scoring, with a person able to review every answer.
  • Dashboards for recruiters, managers and client campaigns, and a scorecard for candidates.
  • A setup for many clients, with onboarding for each one, and a super admin view of usage.

I also planned a dashboard for each role. Admins track logins per day, how many assessments people create, the share graded by AI, and the cost per assessment. Managers track team scores, the spread of scores and skill gaps. Teammates see their own score over time and their recent feedback.

One result, three people

One AI-scored result, three people reading it. The candidate asks: how did I do? The assessor asks: do I trust this score enough to stand behind it? The manager asks: is this helping my team?

The first prototypes made it clear that reporting was the real value, and stakeholders confirmed it. So I designed each view around that person's next decision, not around the data available. Above all three sits the admin, who sets the rules for each campaign.

Version one: start simple, on purpose

My usual approach is to launch a first version with the priority features only, simple enough to use without help. More comes as the product grows with its users.

In version one, an admin writes up to 15 questions, sets a time limit for each one, and sends the test. The candidate answers in a chat. The AI scores the answers.

Key design decisions in version one

1. One wizard for every assessment

Creating an assessment takes four steps: Basic Information, Script, Configure and Scoping. The page saves as you go. I worked through seven rounds of this flow. It started as a modal with a dark side rail. But I knew the flow would grow: more settings, and settings inside settings. In a modal, that would get crowded, especially on small screens. So I gave it its own page, with a stepper at the top. That choice paid off later: the AI version fitted into the same wizard by changing one step.

2. Three ways to add questions

Select a saved script, create one in place, or upload a text or Excel file from a template. Each question has its own time limit, and the footer counts the questions as you add them.

3. Admins set the rules, managers work inside them

Skill weights must add up to 100%. Next to every parameter is an "Allow Edit" box, so the admin decides what a manager can change in one assessment. In the wizard, locked skills show in grey with a short note: admins set skill parameters, and a change can need permission. Admins also choose which statistics the AI reports, from grammar mistakes and readability to tone, empathy and intent.

4. Send by team structure or by email

Scoping works in two ways. Internal teams pick a LOB, a role, a manager and teammates, then confirm the list of candidates. External hiring pastes or uploads candidate emails. After sending, a summary shows how many went out and how many failed, with a Try Again button for the failed ones.

5. Tell candidates everything before they start

The instructions page lists what they need: a quiet space for about 15 minutes, a stable connection, and a headset with a microphone. It explains screen capture and the anti-cheating checks in plain words, and asks for consent before the test starts. During the test, a progress bar and a timer stay in view.

6. Show the AI's working

An AI score is only useful if the assessor can trust it. In the review screen, the AI underlines each mistake in the answer. A side panel lists every correction by type: punctuation, spelling ("u" to "you", "Sory" to "Sorry") and tense ("is" to "was"). The assessor can check a score in seconds, without reading every answer twice.

7. Results that every role can read

Each assessment has a page with its candidates, their status, their score and a level, for example "85%, B2". Each client sets its own levels for its own needs. The page also shows a completion ring and a skills breakdown. Each candidate has a history page. Reports filter by campaign, LOB, site and date, and can go out by email.

8. The states people skip

An expired link or a missing permission gets a friendly page with an illustration and one clear way back. Empty tables and empty pages have their own designs, so nobody lands on a broken screen.

The idea I kept bringing back

While I designed the version one screens, I kept looking at the create flow. Admins had to enter every question and answer by hand. Any standard AI model could do that work.

So I proposed a different model. Give the AI a story, a scenario, as the evaluation. The AI makes the questions, with some randomness, so the test is not the same every time. It can also follow up on the candidate's previous answer, to test the tagged skills from more than one side.

In a call, I asked our senior director of development, politely, if we could build it. I gave two reasons. The test would be more dynamic. And without the load of typing every question, more teams would use the product. At the time, he did not see enough in it to change the plan.

Then an internal client came to us with the exact same requirement. Our Wellness team wanted a tool that could run as much on its own as possible, for their health and wellness campaigns. That changed the conversation, and we built it.

Research: what the Wellness team needed

I spoke with the leader of Wellness at TaskUs. I asked how she used the assessment tools on the market, and what she was actually looking for.

One thing she said stayed with me. She wanted the context for every field on the form itself, while she created an assessment. That made sense. We cannot expect every user to know everything. So each field carries its own brief: how it works, and what the outcome will be.

She also wanted AI help at every step, from writing the scenario to configuring the assessment, so the user never feels lost. Her idea of simplicity through an AI assistant changed how the AI version came in. For her team, we also split scenario creation into more steps, so each step asks for one thing.

Version two: write the scenario, not the questions

The update notes in the file list the changes in five lines. "Mock Call" became a new type in step 1. Step 2 picks a scenario instead of a script, and shows a transcript instead of a question list. Scenario setup replaced script creation, with a test call. Upload became optional. Then came the candidate's side of the call.

1. Describe the situation in plain words

A scenario has a name, a call type (inbound or outbound), an AI voice, a language and a short setup: the scenario, the customer's role and a backstory. For example: "The Rainy Day. Role: Annoyed Rider." The rider was on a ride-hail trip in Austin when a heavy rainstorm hit, and their autonomous car has stopped in the street.

2. Test the call before any candidate does

Start Simulation turns the admin into the agent. The AI customer speaks, and the transcript builds live on the screen. Then the admin can restart, duplicate and edit, or save. Nobody has to guess how a scenario will behave.

3. Show what the AI is doing

A voice call has nothing on screen to watch, so a small status chip shows the AI's state: Idle, Listening, Understanding, Speaking or On Hold. The person on the call always knows whose turn it is.

4. Make the mock call feel like a call

The candidate sees the scenario (if the admin allows it), the status chip and the live transcript. They can put the customer on hold, resume, and end the call, as they would on the floor.

5. Skills that match the test

For a mock call, the skills change from grammar and spelling to the parts of a good call: opening and greeting, acknowledging the issue, empathy and assurance, resolution, and closing with next steps. The same rule applies: the weights add up to 100%.

6. The AI writes the feedback questions too

The admin writes the scenario, and the AI writes the evaluation questions from it. Results get a second tab, "Feedback by Question". Each question gets a score out of 10 and one line on why. For example: "Did the agent acknowledge that the rider felt unsafe before solving the problem?" The tab only shows when the evaluation has feedback questions.

7. A results page built for coaching

Each mock call gets its own results page. Three scores sit at the top: communication, problem solving and customer empathy. Below them are the call recording on a timeline, and the full transcript with time stamps. A summary lists where the agent earned points and where they lost them. Coaching tips close the page, so the next step is always clear.

8. One dashboard for two kinds of test

The analytics page filters by campaign and by product type. When you look at all types together, the skills chart greys out. It comes back when you pick one type, because a language test and a mock call do not measure the same skills.

From design to build

I left notes for developers inside the file, next to the screens they describe. For example: when a simulation starts, scroll to the scenario picker. Only the transcript scrolls, not the whole page. The footer stays at the bottom. "Select all" has three states: all, some and none. The back button only shows for admins who came from the candidate list.

To get the full context of the application, I also wrote 13 points for the team, from Ping sign-in for internal users to resending a link to one candidate.

The developers built the scenario builder straight from the design architecture I set up. Then I tested it from the UI side and improved it. The builder grew to eight steps for the Wellness team: an overview, AI and human roles with objectives and non-negotiable rules, a session flow in phases, a persona builder, if-else behaviour rules, feedback scoring, a summary, and publish. Sliders tune the AI customer's tone, impatience, starting mood, difficulty and age group.

Craft

EvaluateUs uses one type family, Inter, with a short scale: 24, 20 and 16 px for headings, and 16, 14 and 12 px for body text. Components carry their states: step status (completed, current, skipped and upcoming), navigation (expanded and collapsed), menu items (default, hover, selected and disabled), and assessment cards tagged by type. EvaluateUs also got its own theme, so TaskUs could sell it to external clients. It was built to WCAG 2.2 AA throughout.

Outcome

Agents who took EvaluateUs assessments became 17% more accurate in their knowledge of the client campaign.

The first version went live in December 2025, and the AI scenario version went into testing in February 2026. Both went live, one after the other, for different clients. The Wellness team got the tool they asked for: one that runs as much on its own as possible.

What I learned

Being early is not the same as being wrong. The scenario idea made sense from the start, but the business needed a reason to build it. When the reason came, the product was ready, because the first version left room for it. The four-step wizard took the AI version by changing one step, not by starting again.

Ask with the user's cost in mind. I did not ask for AI because it was new. I asked because typing every question is a load on the people who use the product, and a lighter load brings more users.

Good ideas also come from the people you design for. The Wellness leader's idea of context on every field and AI help at every step made the AI version simpler than my first plan.
